On the 13th of August (2026) I attended a meeting with my supervisor Prof. Victor Calo to get an evaluation of my progress so far and ask for feedback. A digitisation of my notes from the meeting can be seen in *Artefact: ```Meeting_Notes_2026_08_13.md```*, and will be implicitly referenced by much of the following. 

During the meeting with Prof. Calo, the primary point of feedback he gave was encouraging the incorporation of large language models (LLMs) into my workflow. Prof. Calo correctly noted that I have not been using LLMs to produce much code, even boiler-plate or test code (*Teamwork 3*). He also acknowledged however that it was something he intentionally didn't push too hard on for the benefit of my learning and understanding, especially when approaching a completely new area of study.

Until recently, LLMs have been something I specifically avoided using to produce code to ensure that I learnt programming concepts with genuine depth. I would also refrain from using LLMs for research where possible, favouring the more exploratory experience of search engines. Previously, my primary use of LLMs was for debugging code. 

Coincidentally, two weeks before my meeting with Prof. Calo I engaged in my first project where I relied on LLMs heavily to produce code while competing in UNSW FinTech's and Susquehanna International Group's Algothon (2026) (*Artefact: ```algothon_screenshot_edited.png```*). With a short time period and only the top 10 teams receiving any tangible benefit, the boost to development speed provided by LLMs outweighed any concerns regarding human understanding or long-term project fragility. However, I deemed that similar reasoning could not be translated to this project as long-term considerations are crucial for public repository contributions and future repository maintenance (*Planning and Organisation 3*).

During the meeting Prof. Calo gave me a number of insights into how he incorporates LLMs into his work. Prof. Calo's advice and examples radically changed the way I thought about using LLMs, especially for programming, as well as prompting me to reflect after regarding where I'd like to direct my future. Prof. Calo's insights included (explicitly and implicitly):
- Do not use LLMs for tasks where the output cannot be validated, particularly via tests.
- LLMs are particularly proficient at writing Python code and produce more "silly" errors and decisions when writing in languages such as C++ and Julia.
- Boiler-plate, writing out tests, and reproducing specifications are not where my value is as a programmer/researcher and an area where LLMs excel.
- The total sum of time and effort needed to understand all or even a fraction of the topics I encounter in this project is far beyond what someone can surmount in a decade, much less a year. I must be selective about the topics I do (e.g. refinement algorithms, T8code, Gridap.jl) and don't (e.g. GMSH, FEM intricacies) dedicate myself to deeply understanding. LLMs can be useful when I need to interface (in known ways) with the latter.
- A balance of learning and productivity can be sometimes found in having LLMs interrogate your ideas and code early on.

Discussing how Prof. Calo uses LLMs in his work encouraged me to reflect on my future in high-performance computing and mathematics. Using LLMs has made it clear to me that simply efficient production of code will no longer be a skill of significant value in the future in the face of LLMs. Instead, I believe that deep domain-specialised understanding will be more valued in the future of high-performance computing and mathematics. To this end, I'd like to establish my future value as a researcher/programmer by specialising deeply in a mathematical domain (*Use of Tools and Technology 2*).
