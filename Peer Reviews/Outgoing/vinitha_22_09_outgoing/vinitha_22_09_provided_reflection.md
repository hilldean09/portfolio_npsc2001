# The Copied Reflection

When I began my SMAAT research project, I did not know exactly which real-world conditions were causing problems for the BlazeFace system. My supervisor initially asked me to investigate whether varying conditions could affect the facial landmark detection, without giving me specific conditions to test. I therefore began with exploratory testing, changing factors such as lighting, head movement and occlusion (Artefact 1 Showing exact testing). I also tested situations where a light source was positioned behind me, creating shadows across my face, as I thought this could reflect situations children might naturally experience when being recorded (Artefact 2 Showing exact testing). 
 
At this stage, my analysis was largely subjective. I visually observed the facial landmarks and identified when their positions appeared inconsistent or incorrectly allocated (Artefact 3 BlazeFace on my testing videos). Some initial tests appeared to show no problems, including a point where I recorded a dark screen because I could not see an obvious error. However, by expanding the range of conditions I tested, I began identifying patterns. Darker lighting produced more variation, overexposure caused incorrect landmark placement, occlusion affected detection, and changes in the position of the light source could create shadows that affected the landmarks (Artefact 4 BlazeFace on my testing videos with bad lighting). 
 
These observations helped me turn a broad research question into a more structured testing plan. Rather than continuing to test conditions without a clear direction, I began identifying specific variables that could be quantified. My supervisor then guided me to move beyond observing that something “looked wrong” and determine whether the problem could be demonstrated through measurable results. I began designing tests around specific conditions, such as the angle of head movement under controlled lighting, so that limitations could be identified numerically (Artefact 5 Email with descriptions of quantifying from Supervisor). 
 
This experience changed how I approach planning research. I previously thought an action plan needed to be established before beginning the investigation. I now understand that exploratory work can be an important first step in developing an effective plan, particularly when the problem is not fully understood. The process taught me to use initial observations to determine what should be investigated systematically, rather than trying to plan every detail before understanding the problem. 
 
This approach will also be valuable in future research and software development. Quantifying the conditions under which a system fails can turn an observation into an actionable limitation, such as identifying when an input should be treated as an outlier or prompting the program to respond differently. It has strengthened my ability to map an initially broad idea into specific, measurable and achievable actions.
