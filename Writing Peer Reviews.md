The reflection relates to my outgoing peer reviews which can be found, as a form of artefact, in the ```./Peer Reviews/Outgoing/``` directory.

In writing peer reviews on my classmates' reflections I found a new awareness of my preferences and biases as an audience, and had the opportunity to practice questioning their validity. 

While reviewing my classmates' reflection I found that the primary points that either weakened or strengthened my perception of the work was whether it incorporated the rubric requirements and how specific the language used was. My process for reviewing my peer's work involved recording dot points, first regarding the rubric and then more general feedback, and then writing the full review. A common section seen in my dot points was "Rubric Pros" and "Rubric Cons" where I evaluated how well I thought my peers' reflection matched with the rubric (common notes included a lack of explicit connection to artefacts and a lack of explicit connection to career growth). Another common note in my dot points was regarding a lack of concrete examples and connections between outcomes and changes. I feeling I found myself having was a weaker evaluation the more "this" was used without having being introduced within the same sentence.

As I wrote more reviews I wanted to evaluate whether or not my review structure was reasonable and helpful. A particular question I kept considering was whether or not there was value in grounding a large portion of my review in the Portfolio rubric, which my peers also have access to, obviously it is optimal if the goal is purely grades, but on the contrary, does it also support their personal growth? I believe that explicit examples are very valuable wherever possible as both a narrative tool and a tool for directing the audiences qualitative and quantitative evaluation, thus I felt comfortable including feedback encouraging it. Finally, I found myself questioning, entirely introspectively, my subconscious tendency to favour pieces which did not use "this" vaguely. I came to the conclusion that the latter two biases likely originate from reading and writing a large amount of mathematics and physics content, where implicit definition is strongly avoided. Regarding the first point, I settled on the idea that a balance of rubric focused and general-growth feedback is still, likely, non-trivially beneficial overall.
