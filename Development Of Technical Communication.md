Facing technical challenges that confront core paradigms within finite-element codes (```T8code```, ```p4est```, ```Gridap.jl```) and algorithms has necessitated the development of my technical communication skills. I have previously identified technical communication, particularly in a team, as a skill I needed more experience in (*Teamwork 1 and 2*).

While implementing a topology based global vertex enumeration algorithm I came across a feature of ```T8code```, neighbour face orientation encoding, for which I could only assume my understanding was correct, but not yet confirm. Despite multiple avenues of resolution I still could not definitively confirm the accuracy of my understanding. I continued programming under the assumption my understanding was correct, which I would then later test after the needed functionality was established. 

Shortly after I attended a Zoom meeting with my supervisor, Dr. Alberto Martin of the Australian National University, to provide an update on my progress. During our meeting I addressed the aforementioned uncertainty, and some odd behaviour produced by the later tests hoping to learn from Dr. Martin's experience with finite-element codes. It quickly became apparent however that I lacked the terminology to concisely explain the issue. I stumbled over explanations for a while trying to preserve some sense of succinctness to no avail. After approximately ten minutes, I abandoned succinctness and presented the issue in a verbose but unambiguous mathematical framework. The piece of terminology that encapsulated my issue was found to be "cyclic shift."

After the three-hour meeting, during which my capacity to formalise my ideas into concise technical statements was thoroughly tested, I reflected on the experience; I arrived at the following conclusions, benefiting my technical communication skills:
1) The verbosity of rigorous mathematical statements lends to itself a simplicity that can be more universally relied upon during technical discussions where more concise terminology is not available.
2) The consultation of an expert on a topic is greatly limited by one's own capacity to accurately communicate their questions.
3) Simple drawing applications can be very valuable during remote meetings for technical discussions. Having a drawing software you are familiar with can be a significant help in reducing the cognitive load of the other attendees during a technical explanations.

I found the first conclusion in particular challenged my preconception that learning a variety of concise field-specific jargon should be highly valued. While I still believe in the applicability of jargon to reduce cognitive load during discussion, I believe I should place more value on becoming proficient in structuring ideas into precise mathematical statements. 

In a high-performance computing and/or mathematically focused career, I am likely to eventually come across a complex idea for which no concise jargon exists yet. For situations like the aforementioned, being able to unambiguously and accessibly present the idea to a team from different backgrounds can greatly benefit collaborative advancement by increasing the number of people who accurately understand the idea. To achieve this, fluency in the multidisciplinary language of mathematics will be valuable.
